# Readings: 9 comparison pages by SaaS leaders

Public "<Brand> vs <Rival>" pages on the vendors' own sites, read in one pass and coded in
`data/section_library.py` (section type ids in brackets). Notes are paraphrased: they describe structure, section
order and claim patterns, not the page copy. Pages change: re-read the live page before you capture it or describe
it in a deliverable, and never quote from these notes.

Each entry: address and format, how the pair is named, the sections in order, what stands out, and the caution.

## HubSpot vs Salesforce
- hubspot.com/comparisons/salesforce-vs-hubspot (landing page in a comparisons library). Own brand first in the H1;
  the H1 uses the rival's current product name with the legacy name in brackets; the slug puts the rival first.
- Order: hero with a one-line tagline and two buttons (free start, demo) > three switcher-stat cards tied to an
  internal survey > a checkmark table with a show-more toggle > three differentiator blocks that reuse the stat-card
  titles > three switcher quotes and a find-a-partner button > a row of review-site badges > a seven-question FAQ >
  related product cards > a survey footnote with sample size and month.
- Stands out: switcher-outcome stats sourced to a dated customer survey with a disclosed sample size, placed above
  the table so proof lands before feature claims [6, 8, 34]. A coexistence answer (two-way sync with the rival) sits
  in the FAQ [41, 43].
- Caution: no row where the rival is equal or better; the only concession is in a customer's voice. No date, author
  or closing band.

## Zendesk vs Freshdesk
- zendesk.com/why-zendesk/zendesk-vs-freshdesk/ (landing page). Own brand first.
- Order: hero with a two-sentence competitive subhead and two buttons > a last-updated date under the hero image >
  why-switch intro with four numbered pillars > a text table grouped under the same four pillars > five feature
  deep-dives with product screenshots and one analyst statistic > a two-column price table > three switcher story
  cards with metrics > a three-question FAQ > closing band with review-site badges > ten related comparisons and an
  alternatives link.
- Stands out: narrative and table share one framework (the four pillars) [11, 15]; the price table states each
  side's billing basis [22]; a visible updated date [5].
- Caution: concessions are backhanded; badges carry no scores, counts or dates.

## monday.com vs Asana
- monday.com/blog/project-management/asana-vs-monday-com-which-to-choose/ (blog article; the vendor's alternatives
  hub routes every vs link to a blog post). Rival named first in the title and H1, year in the title.
- Order: byline with photo, updated date, read time, intro and two buttons > table of contents > five key takeaways
  (one is a rival strength) > an at-a-glance text table whose first row is Best for > twelve dimension sections, each
  H2 written as "dimension: verdict", two of them parity verdicts > paired go-with-X-if and go-with-Y-if lists >
  eight related comparisons > conclusion with a button > a seven-question FAQ.
- Stands out: the most balanced page in the set. Every dimension heading carries its verdict, so the headings alone
  give the answer [14, 15]; Best for as the first table row [11]; paired choose-if lists [17]; review-site scores
  with review counts for both [32]; a FAQ answer on connecting the two products [41].
- Caution: no reviewer or method; prices tier by tier go stale fast.

## Asana vs monday.com
- asana.com/compare/asana-vs-monday-com (landing page in /compare). Own brand first.
- Order: hero with positioning paragraph and two buttons > customer-count trust line with logos and an as-of
  footnote > a six-row included / not-available grid > review-site quotes that name the rival plus case-study quotes
  > four feature tabs > analyst and review-site recognition > an ROI calculator framed as justifying the switch > a
  large FAQ in five tabs > closing band.
- Stands out: the grid concedes parity on half its rows, which makes the remaining rows more credible [12, 16]; an
  FAQ built at scale around buyer concerns, many questions naming the rival [43]; a calculator [25].
- Caution: copy is one-sided; recognition claims carry no years.

## ClickUp vs Asana
- clickup.com/compare/asana-vs-clickup (short landing page). Own brand in the H1, rival first in the title and slug.
- Order: hero with eyebrow, subhead and one button > review-site score with review count > logo strip > a one-
  paragraph intro > a long checkmark grid > footer with links to every vs page and security badges.
- Stands out: radical brevity (about 500 words) and a sitewide footer block linking every comparison [12, 45].
- Caution: no summary, pricing, FAQ, concession or date.

## Notion vs Confluence
- notion.com/product/notion-vs-confluence (switch landing page; a second canonical address exists). The H1 names
  only the rival, framed as moving beyond it.
- Order: hero with two buttons > switcher logos > three one-line pain points > an AI block > review-site badges and
  one switcher quote > four own-product boxes > a demo > resource cards including a downloadable comparison guide >
  a five-question FAQ > a closing band naming the importer.
- Stands out: ties the switch to the rival's own forcing event (an end-of-life for one of its editions) and pairs
  it with a named importer and a sales-led migration assessment [38, 41]; the detailed comparison is pushed into a
  downloadable guide [47].
- Caution: no side-by-side table and no concession.

## Airtable vs Smartsheet
- airtable.com/airtable-vs-smartsheet (landing page at root level). Own brand first.
- Order: hero with an unsourced ranked-higher-by-customers line and two buttons > logos > three customer metrics >
  positioning copy > a two-column text comparison > four differentiators > review-site quotes, two naming the rival >
  long-form narrative > five question-led H2s (product equivalents, alternatives, integrations, price, trial) >
  closing band.
- Stands out: a question from a current rival user answered with a module-by-module equivalence map, which settles
  "what replaces what" [41, 43].
- Caution: the hero claim has no source; no date, author or footnotes.

## Intercom vs Zendesk
- intercom.com/compare-intercom-vs-zendesk (landing page at root level). Own brand first.
- Order: hero with an outcome-based price and two buttons > customer-count logo strip > three switch reasons >
  capability blocks > a five-row yes/no grid > AI-performance cards > own pricing only > four quotes (two from
  switchers, one on migration) > a six-question FAQ > closing band.
- Stands out: a no-rip-and-replace path (its AI agent works on top of the rival's helpdesk) plus an FAQ asking
  whether it can be used without migrating [41, 43]; this is the coexistence pattern.
- Caution: every grid row is yes versus no; no third-party proof; the headline resolution figure has no footnote.

## Webflow vs WordPress
- webflow.com/vs/wordpress (landing page in /vs). The H1 names only the rival, framed as an alternative.
- Order: hero with a contact button and a total-cost-estimate button > logos > CMS tabs by persona > four why-us
  blocks > a gated migration guide > four named quotes > a sixteen-row, three-way table (the rival, the rival's
  enterprise tier, the brand) with an icon plus a sentence in every cell > a business-impact calculator > a six-
  question FAQ > closing band with support commitments.
- Stands out: the table includes the rival's enterprise tier and concedes parity where it exists [11, 12, 16]; the
  FAQ takes the plugin-ecosystem objection head on [43]; migration guide and calculator [25, 41, 47].
- Caution: no third-party proof and no date.

## Patterns across the 9
- Two templates. Eight are product landing pages, most in a dedicated folder (/compare, /comparisons, /vs and
  similar): short, one-sided, many buttons. One is a blog article: longer, with a byline, one section per dimension,
  verdicts in the headings, and the only real concessions in the set.
- Typical landing-page order: hero (H1 naming both brands, claim-led subhead, two buttons) > logos or trust line >
  three or four why-switch pillars or stats > comparison table > feature deep-dives > quotes, often from switchers >
  review-site badges > FAQ > closing band > sometimes related comparisons.
- Near-universal: own brand first in the H1 (6 of 9), a comparison table (8 of 9), a button in the hero (9 of 9), an
  FAQ (8 of 9).
- Tables: formats split between yes/no grids, text cells and icon plus sentence. No table shows the rival ahead on
  any row; parity rows appear on 3.
- Pricing: 3 of 9 show the rival's prices; one describes the rival's plans in words; the rest show only their own
  price, a calculator or nothing.
- Third-party proof: review-site badges are the default, mostly without review counts or dates. Analyst proof
  appears on 2. Two cite no third party at all.
- Customer proof leans on switchers: 4 of 9 show named customers who left the rival.
- Migration is mostly an FAQ answer (7 of the 8 FAQs ask a move or switch question). Dedicated blocks are rare (a
  gated guide, an equivalence map, an importer). Some offer to run beside the rival instead.
- Freshness and authorship are weak: 2 of 9 show an updated date, 1 an author, none a reviewer or a method.
- FAQ: the first question is usually the head-to-head query itself; rival-news questions (an edition being retired)
  capture extra demand.
- Related comparisons appear on 4 of 9; calculators on 2.
