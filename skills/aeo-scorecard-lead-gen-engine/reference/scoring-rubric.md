# Scoring rubric: the 7 pillars

Each pillar is scored 0 to 100 against a target of **70**. The composite is the roll-up: a simple
average is defensible and keeps the sheet and the report consistent; weight per category once you
have volume. Scores are **judgement anchored to evidence**, not a formula. Every score must be
defensible with one specific fact; if you can't name the evidence, don't assign the number.

## 1. Citation share: "How often AI recommends you"
How often the brand is the named source in AI answers to category buyer prompts. Evidence: named
count / total probes, plus the competitor leaderboard. Anchors: 0 to 20 = about 1 in 10 or fewer, or
local-only; 40 to 60 = shows up on some category queries; 70+ = regularly named on generic category
questions. This is share of answers, not traffic and not market share; say so.

## 2. Content structure: "Can AI read your site easily"
Whether pages are structured for extraction. Evidence: on-page score, heading hierarchy, schema
types and completeness, page count, duplicate or legacy sites. Anchors: 0 to 30 broken, thin or no
schema; about 50 valid basics (Organization / WebSite / BreadcrumbList) but gaps and/or a split site;
70+ clean structure plus Service / FAQPage / Article schema.

## 3. Answer readiness: "Do your pages give direct answers"
Whether a model can lift a clean, citable passage. Evidence: direct answer blocks, FAQ/Q&A,
definitions, comparisons; the ratio of prose to answers. Anchors: 0 to 30 pure marketing copy;
about 50 answers exist but are buried in long, unmarked prose; 70+ answer-first pages with FAQ or
HowTo markup. Watch: explainer content is not enough if it is unmarked long-form. The
typical mid-score line: "you wrote the answers; they just can't be lifted."

## 4. Competitor gaps: "Questions competitors own"
Queries competitors win where the brand is absent. Evidence: the probe set (which prompts named a
rival and not the brand); missing page types (comparison hubs, "best [category]" pages, segment
hubs). A low score means big gaps: 0 to 30 = absent on every generic category query.

## 5. Freshness: "How current your content is"
Evidence: last-modified dates, blog cadence, current-year assets. 60+ = an active cadence. Don't
mark it low by reflex: it's often a genuine strength, and saying so buys credibility for the harsh
scores elsewhere.

## 6. Entity clarity: "Is your brand one clear identity"
Whether engines resolve the brand as one entity. Evidence: Organization schema completeness
(`sameAs`, address, `contactPoint`), name variants, duplicate or legacy sites, authoritative records
(Wikidata, a clean LinkedIn page), whether a live search even finds their profiles. Anchors: 0 to 35
incomplete schema plus name variants plus a split site; 70+ one clean, linked entity. Name variants
and split sites are concrete, usually cheap fixes: always call them out.

## 7. Off-site mentions: "Who else talks about you"
Presence in the offsite sources engines read. Evidence: authority score, referring domains,
directory, review and press presence, community mentions. 0 to 30 low authority with thin reviews and
press; 70+ broadly referenced. **Look for untapped assets** (sponsorships, network memberships, awards
that never became citable mentions): they make the fix concrete and cheap.

## Turning scores into a story
- Lead with the **aggregate**, never by counting example cards.
- The strongest narrative for most established brands: "It's not a content problem; it's a
  machine-readability and authority problem." It is usually true, it respects the work they've done,
  and it points at a fix.
- Pair every low score with the specific evidence and a fix. A number without a receipt is arguable;
  a receipt is not.
- Competitor rates can add up to more than 100%: one answer can name several brands. Share of voice
  is a ratio, not a pie.
- You can't compare two brands' composites head to head: each is scored on prompts generated from
  its own positioning. The same brand over time is fine.
