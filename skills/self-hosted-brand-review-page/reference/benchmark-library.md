# Benchmark library: self-hosted review pages and the pages they compete with

The named pages below are public examples, studied for their structure only. Re-check any page
before citing it in a client deliverable: pages move, get redirected or lose their rankings.
Paraphrase what a page does; never copy its text.

## Who ranks for "<brand> review(s)": what to record

For each top-20 result on the brand's review, reviews, alternatives, pricing and worth-it
queries, record the owner type:

- **Competitor**: a vendor that sells a competing product, reviewing the brand on its own site.
- **Affiliate or independent**: bloggers, agencies, review sites; note any affiliate disclosure.
- **Review platform**: G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Gartner Peer Insights, Product Hunt and similar.
- **UGC**: Reddit and other forums, creator posts on LinkedIn, YouTube reviews.
- **Employer reviews**: Glassdoor and similar sites (they match "reviews" queries too).
- **Brand**: any page on the brand's own site, and whether a `/reviews` URL exists at all.

Also note SERP features: AI Overview, People Also Ask, video pack, forums block. The mix varies by
brand and category; count it rather than assuming it.

Vendor-owned review pages (status when checked; re-check before citing):

| Vendor page | Ranks for its own review query? |
|---|---|
| clickup.com/reviews | Yes, page one |
| keap.com/compare (live G2 widget showing the real score) | Yes, page one, for "keap reviews" |

Many well-known SaaS brands have no vendor page in the top 25 for their own review query.

Read: a vendor page can rank for its own review query, and most do not. The ones that rank tend to
be old, heavily linked hubs. A testimonial wall with no byline, no date and no negatives rarely
ranks even for its own name. The format this skill builds (a blog-style page with a disclosed
bias, a byline, dates and real pushback) borrows the traits third-party reviews use to rank.

## Default page order (adapt per client, keep the first four)

1. H1 that names the bias (for example "<Brand> reviews: what users say, from a source that isn't
   neutral") and a two-line intro saying who wrote it and how quotes were picked
2. TL;DR verdict: who it is for, who it is not for, the top two praises and the top two complaints
3. Scoreboard: each review platform, score, review count, "as of" date, link to the full profile
4. What users praise, in 3 to 5 job-to-be-done clusters, each quote traceable
5. Where users push back: the recurring complaints from the platforms and from page-one competitor
   reviews, each answered with what has changed and when (date it)
6. What it costs and when it pays off
7. Creators and community: LinkedIn posts, YouTube reviews, Reddit threads
8. Customer stories: outcome stat plus a link to each story
9. Who it is for and not for (can merge into 2 if short)
10. FAQ from People Also Ask and grounded question tools
11. Method and disclosures: how quotes were chosen, platform disclaimers, links to every full profile
12. CTA in the article's normal CTA slot

A "reviews you will find elsewhere" section that names competitor-written reviews is a client
call: many clients ban naming or linking competitors. Without the names it still works, for
example by stating how many of the ranking reviews are published by companies that sell competing
tools.
